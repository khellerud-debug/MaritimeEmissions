IMO DCS and EU MRV are both annual fuel-and-activity collectors. The same fuel log feeds the CII rating and the EU ETS allowance calculation and the FuelEU intensity calc. One fuel input — three regulatory consequences.

The Offshore Gap

IMO CII uses transport work (mass × distance) — offshore vessels don't transport, they station-keep. DNV's EMRED JIP framework fills this gap with a 5-mode metric using GT or bollard pull as denominator. Industry consensus, not compliance.

Net-Zero Limbo

The IMO Net-Zero Framework was approved at MEPC 83 (Apr 2025) but its formal adoption was adjourned at MEPC ES/2 (Oct 2025). MEPC 84 (Apr 2026) showed renewed support. Next vote: Dec 2026. Entry into force ~16 months later — so 2028 if adopted.

Why offshore is "Mostly N/A" for CII

CII's denominator is capacity × distance — Annual Efficiency Ratio (AER) for cargo, modified forms for cruise. Offshore vessels spend large portions of their time in DP, standby, or anchor handling — operations with zero transport work. Dividing emissions by zero or near-zero distance yields meaningless ratings. This is the gap DNV's 5-mode framework was built to fill.

The 400-GT review window

By 31 Dec 2026, the EU Commission must report on extending EU ETS to offshore and general-cargo ships in the 400–5000 GT band — and to ships below 400 GT. Today these are MRV-only, but their EU ETS inclusion sits inside the 2026 review cycle. The 400 GT segment is the next domino.

ECA ≠ EU ETS — two different geographic concepts that overlap but follow different rules

ECA geometry (IMO MARPOL Annex VI) — dictates what fuel you burn: 0.10% sulphur inside an ECA vs 0.50% globally. Every ship in the area is subject to this rule. Comply by switching to low-S fuel or using an exhaust gas cleaning system (scrubber) with equivalent SOx reduction.

EU ETS geometry (Directive 2003/87/EC as amended) — dictates who pays for CO2. Applies to voyages arriving at or departing from an EU/EEA port — regardless of whether the water is also an ECA.

Example: Angola → Rotterdam pays EU ETS on 50% of the voyage (the extra-EEA leg), but only switches to ECA-compliant fuel once it enters the English Channel / North Sea SECA.

Can you allocate biofuel only to EU legs? Mostly no — not by paperwork alone. Under EU ETS and FuelEU, fuel consumption is tracked per voyage through MRV (Implementing Regulation 2016/1928, Annex II). The owner reports actual fuel consumed by type on each voyage segment. You can, however, physically bunker / use different fuels on different segments — the same way ships switch from VLSFO to MGO when entering a SOx ECA. With separate tanks or a fuel-changeover, an owner can run biofuel only on EU legs and HFO on extra-EU legs — and the MRV log will reflect that. The Mass-Balance approach (RED-II Art. 30) lets accredited biofuel suppliers chain-of-custody the volumes; that's what makes the zero-rating defensible. Pure paper allocation without physical use is not permitted.

The fuel-economics inversion (2026 onwards)

At Q1 2026 prices, B30 on an intra-EU voyage delivers a $128/mt cost advantage over straight VLSFO once ETS surrender + FuelEU compliance are netted out. On an extra-EU voyage (50% ETS scope) B30 is still close to break-even. The headline biofuel "premium" is misleading because it ignores the avoided EUA + FuelEU costs. This is the inversion most cost-stack tools don't show.

What this tool doesn't model (but matters)

  • · Charterer/owner split — under most BIMCO clauses, ETS and FuelEU costs pass through to the charterer; the owner's exposure depends on contract structure
  • · RFNBO multiplier sunset — renewable fuels of non-biological origin count 2× toward FuelEU intensity until end-2033; from 2034 the cliff doubles the apparent shortfall
  • · Biofuel supply — advanced biofuels (UCO, residues) are RED-II compliant but supply is constrained; non-RED biofuels don't get the zero-rating treatment
  • · OPS at port — mandatory at TEN-T ports for container/passenger from 2030; eliminates berth emissions but requires shore-side investment
  • · Methane slip in LNG dual-fuel — from 2026 CH₄/N₂O enter ETS via the global warming potential factor; older 2-stroke Otto engines lose much of LNG's TtW advantage

TtW vs WtW

EU ETS prices Tank-to-Wake CO₂ (and CH₄, N₂O from 2026). FuelEU measures Well-to-Wake GHG intensity per MJ of energy used. Same molecule, different boundaries — a fuel can be cheap on one and expensive on the other. Bio-LNG saves on FuelEU more than on ETS; HVO saves on both. The arbitrage is real.

The Net-Zero Framework wildcard

If adopted Dec 2026 and in force ~2028, the IMO GFI mechanism adds a third pricing layer globally. Industry expectations centre on a Tier-2 deficit price near $100/tCO₂e. The EU has signalled willingness to recalibrate ETS/FuelEU to avoid double-pricing, but no mechanism is yet defined.

Who actually pays?

Both ETS and FuelEU name the shipping company (typically the ISM manager) as the compliance entity. But voyage costs are usually charterers' — leading to EUA / FuelEU cost clauses in time and voyage charters. BIMCO has standard clauses for both; disputes are now appearing where charterers refused EUA transfers.

Compliance arbitrage opportunities (and pitfalls)

FuelEU pooling

Bio-LNG carriers can sell surplus compliance balance to fossil-fuel ships in the same pool. A literal compliance-unit market is forming — Anthony Veder LNG ships joined Gasum's pool as surplus generators.

RFNBO 2x window

Through end-2033, every MJ of e-fuel counts double toward FuelEU. A ship running 5% RFNBO acts like it's running 10%. After 2033 the multiplier collapses to 1× — a one-time arbitrage.

Banking & borrowing

Over-compliant ships bank surplus into next year; under-compliant can borrow up to 2% from next year (with 10% penalty). Borrowing two years running is barred — a soft cap on deficit-rolling.

OPS exemption from FuelEU

2025–2029: shore-power energy at berth is zero-rated in FuelEU intensity calc — a clean win. From 2030 OPS becomes mandatory for container & passenger ships at TEN-T ports.

Ice-class 5% reduction

Ice-class ships get a 5% reduction in EU ETS surrender obligation — minor relief, often forgotten in cost models.

EU port evasion guardrails

Container transhipment ports within 300 nm of EU/EEA (East Port Said, Tangier-Med) count as EU port calls — to prevent route-shuffling to avoid ETS.

Top LNG watch-outs

The four most commercially decisive things to know before opening the engine tables and intensity bands below.

Methane slip

CH₄ and N₂O enter EU ETS from 1 Jan 2026 via the GWP-100 factor. Engine type and measured slip become commercially decisive — same hull, ±20–30% on the bill.

Tank-to-Wake vs Well-to-Wake

EU ETS prices TtW. FuelEU and IMO NZF price WtW. Bio-LNG looks identical to fossil LNG in TtW but ~80% lower in WtW. Optimise the wrong lens and you leave money on the table.

FuelEU pooling

LNG over-compliers (low-slip ME-GI) generate surplus that can pool with under-compliant operators. Unofficial market price ~€185/tCO₂e vs €400/tCO₂e penalty — the spread is the negotiating room.

RFNBO multiplier cliff

e-LNG and other RFNBO fuels count 2× toward FuelEU compliance until 31 Dec 2033, then 1× from 2034. The cliff has to be priced into multi-year RFNBO investment cases now.

The 2026 Pricing Event — Why LNG Changed Character

Before 1 January 2026, EU ETS priced only CO₂. LNG's lower carbon factor per kWh (about 2.75 tCO₂/t fuel vs 3.114 for VLSFO) gave it a 12–15% headline advantage on the surrender bill. From 2026, CH₄ and N₂O enter the ETS via the GWP-100 conversion factor. Methane has a GWP-100 of 25 (and a GWP-20 of about 85), so even a 1% slip materially changes the WtW intensity score that FuelEU Maritime uses. The same hull running the same cargo can produce a 20–30% different EU bill depending on engine type and slip rate.

FuelEU Maritime measures on a WtW basis from day one (1 January 2025), so the methane question matters there even earlier. The combined pressure means data quality on the methane number is now commercially decisive, not just nice to have.

Engine type matters — methane slip by technology

Methane slip is heavily engine-type-dependent. The two-stroke ME-GI (Diesel cycle) is the lowest-slip option. Two-stroke X-DF (Otto cycle, lean burn) is mid-range. Four-stroke DF (used for auxiliaries and some smaller carriers) is the highest. The choice was largely invisible to commercial parties when only CO₂ was priced; from 2026 it appears directly on the EU bill.

FuelEU intensity bands by fuel

FuelEU sets a WtW intensity target measured in gCO₂e per MJ of energy used. The baseline is VLSFO at 91.16 gCO₂e/MJ. Each year the target tightens. The number that matters for a vessel is how its actual fuel mix compares to the year's target.

Fuel WtW intensity (typical)

  • VLSFO / HFO: ~91.2 gCO₂e/MJ (baseline)
  • MGO: ~90.6 gCO₂e/MJ (essentially same as VLSFO)
  • LNG (low-slip ME-GI): ~76 gCO₂e/MJ
  • LNG (high-slip 4-stroke DF): ~88 gCO₂e/MJ (close to HFO!)
  • B30 blend: ~75 gCO₂e/MJ
  • B100 (RED-II): ~25 gCO₂e/MJ
  • Green methanol: ~5–15 gCO₂e/MJ
  • Green ammonia: ~0–15 gCO₂e/MJ
  • e-LNG / synthetic CH₄: ~5–20 gCO₂e/MJ

FuelEU target (vs 91.16 baseline)

  • 2025: −2% → 89.34 gCO₂e/MJ target
  • 2030: −6% → 85.69 gCO₂e/MJ target
  • 2035: −14.5% → 77.94 gCO₂e/MJ target
  • 2040: −31% → 62.90 gCO₂e/MJ target
  • 2045: −62% → 34.64 gCO₂e/MJ target
  • 2050: −80% → 18.23 gCO₂e/MJ target

Through 2030 a low-slip LNG vessel sits comfortably below the target. From 2035 the maths gets tight, and from 2040 only LNG with a substantial bio- or e-LNG blend (or pooling with cleaner ships) keeps it compliant.

What this means commercially

For LNG carriers built before 2020 (largely X-DF or 4-stroke DF), the 2026 pricing event compresses the commercial advantage against modern HFO designs by roughly 30–50%. For ME-GI newbuilds, the advantage holds — but the burden of proof shifts onto the vessel: the verifier and the charterer both want to see the methane numbers, not just the headline fuel CO₂ factor. Measurement infrastructure becomes commercial infrastructure.

Top Offshore watch-outs

The four most commercially decisive things to know before reading the 5-mode formula and WTIV comparison below.

CII denominator problem

DP and standby time don't fit cargo×distance. Offshore was excluded from IMO CII for exactly this reason — the metric doesn't say what it claims to say for our operating profile.

Crew-change port calls

Directive (EU) 2023/959 brings crew-change ports into the “port of call” definition for offshore. A vessel relieving crew via Stavanger is in ETS scope; same vessel via a non-EEA port may not be.

400–5,000 GT review window

European Commission must review this band by 31 Dec 2026. Many PSVs (~3,500 GT) and AHTS (~3,800 GT) sit in it. Newbuild decisions need to model both outcomes — preserved or extended downward.

IMO NZF as a better offshore fit

NZF's per-MJ denominator (gCO₂e/MJ of energy used) is transport-agnostic. Where CII broke for offshore because of the denominator, NZF might be the first global metric that actually fits.

Why Offshore Needed Its Own CII

IMO's CII formula divides annual CO₂ by capacity × distance sailed. For a tanker that picks up cargo in Houston and unloads in Rotterdam, that ratio is intuitive — tonnes of CO₂ per tonne-mile of cargo moved. For a Platform Supply Vessel that spends 60% of its year in dynamic positioning over an offshore field, the same formula divides by something close to zero. The rating becomes meaningless. So offshore service vessels were excluded from IMO CII entirely.

The DNV-led EMRED JIP (Phase II) brings together major offshore players to agree a methodology that does work for these vessels. Instead of one denominator, it splits the year into five operational modes and assigns each a corrected CO₂-per-time intensity. The output isn't directly comparable to IMO CII letters — it's an internal benchmarking tool for an industry that needed one.

How the offshore segment maps to the wider regulatory landscape

In force

IMO design / engine rules

EEDI doesn't apply to offshore (no transport-work basis). EEXI does apply if ≥400 GT. NOx Tier III applies inside NECAs based on engine install date. SOx 0.10% ECA fuel limit applies always inside ECAs — offshore in the North Sea / Baltic / Mediterranean is fully in scope.

Phasing in

EU regional regime

EU MRV from 1 Jan 2025 for offshore ≥400 GT calling at EEA ports (note: stopping to relieve crew counts as a port call). EU ETS from 1 Jan 2027 for offshore ≥5000 GT — first surrender 30 Sep 2028. FuelEU applies to offshore that meets its scope criteria.

Industry

DNV CII for Offshore

The agreed framework does not have compliance weight — DNV explicitly recommends against using it for compliance because year-on-year variation in operations and charterer demands distorts results. Its role: internal benchmarking and a shared language between operators and charterers.

Regulatory detail behind the watch-outs

Three of the items called out in Top Offshore watch-outs at the top of this deep-dive, expanded with the underlying regulatory mechanics for the people in the room who need the detail.

  • 1.The 400–5000 GT band: today MRV-only, but the EU Commission must report by 31 Dec 2026 on extending ETS to this band. A large slice of the OSV/PSV fleet sits here.
  • 2.The “port of call” definition for offshore: includes the port where the vessel stops to relieve crew (Directive (EU) 2023/959). This widens the EEA emissions catchment considerably for fields with rotating crew changes.
  • 3.The IMO Net-Zero Framework, when in force, will apply to ships ≥5000 GT — including offshore. The GFI methodology has no transport-work problem (it's per MJ of energy), so it covers offshore naturally. A potentially cleaner fit than CII.

Where the differentiation actually shows up

All EEA-operating WTIVs face the same ETS / FuelEU stack from 2027. The real ranking differentiator is fuel intensity (gCO2e/MJ on a WtW basis): the Maersk methanol dual-fuel newbuild and Cadeler's X-class hybrids will outperform legacy diesel-electric peers on FuelEU compliance, banking surplus that can be pooled or sold under Article 21.

The Jones Act side note

Charybdis and other US-flagged WTIVs operating exclusively on US-coast wind farms sit entirely outside the EU stack. But they still face IMO DCS, EEXI, and (when in force) IMO NZF — the global instruments. This makes the US WTIV economics a distinct planning case rather than just a "non-EU" version of the same problem.

Who sets the correction factors?

The factors are not owner-set. EMRED is a Joint Industry Project led by DNV with 14+ industry partners — including Cadeler, Maersk Supply Service, Fred. Olsen Windcarrier, Jan De Nul, DEME, Van Oord, Heerema, Ziton, plus the offshore-wind developers (RWE, Vattenfall, Ørsted, ScottishPower Renewables). Phase 1 (concluded Nov 2023) agreed the framework and operational modes. Phase 2 is testing the framework with broader data collection. Once concluded, the correction factors are common across the industry — which is the whole point of a JIP.

What the ranking tells you

Three things drive the intensity number down: a larger hull (denominator grows with GT), a hybrid / battery configuration (peak-shaves the heavy crane and DP draws), and a lower-carbon fuel (methanol or bio-diesel cuts the gCO₂/MJ factor). The Cadeler X-class wins on all three. Methanol dual-fuel running on green methanol would cut Wind Pace's number by a further 50–70%.

Top Merchant watch-outs

The four most commercially decisive things to know before reading the stack table and charter clauses below.

Cost stacking

An intra-EEA tonne of CO₂ in 2028 can be priced three times: EUA (~€75), FuelEU penalty (€2,400/t VLSFOeq if non-compliant), and IMO NZF remedial unit (~$100/tCO₂e). Strategic models must price the full stack.

Charter allocation

BIMCO ETSA 2022 + FuelEU 2023 clauses pass the cost to charterer. Pre-2022 charters may not; English-law disputes are now testing whether EUAs are “goods” under the Sale of Goods Act.

CII commercial pressure

Regulatorily a soft tool (corrective-action plan only). Commercially a gatekeeper: cargo owners screen on rating, banks discount D/E for green finance, sale-purchase values track ratings.

FuelEU pooling market

Compliance surplus is tradeable across operators. An unofficial market at ~€185/tCO₂e is forming. The spread vs €400/tCO₂e penalty is the negotiating room.

The Mature Regulatory Stack

Merchant tonnage — container, bulk, tanker, general cargo — is the segment for which the regulatory machinery was originally designed. Every instrument in scope assumes “maritime transport activity” means commercial cargo carriage between ports, and that's exactly what merchant ships do. From 2026 the stack is fully phased in for any vessel ≥5,000 GT operating in or via the EEA. The commercial conversation has shifted from “are we in scope?” (yes, fully) to “how do we manage the cost, and who pays?”

Charter-party allocation in practice

The BIMCO standard clauses turn the regulatory bill into a contractual question. Which clauses are in the charter determines who actually pays. Three things to look for in a contract review:

For Voyage Charters

BIMCO EUA Surcharge Clause 2022

Adds an EUA-cost-equivalent surcharge to the freight rate. Standard inclusion since mid-2023; most spot contracts now have it. Without it, owner bears the cost.

For Time Charters

BIMCO ETSA Clause 2022 + FuelEU Clause 2023

Charterer reimburses owner for EUA surrender and FuelEU penalties. Pre-2022 charters likely don't have these — renegotiation is active in the LNG and dry-bulk term markets.

For Bareboat

Charterer holds the DOC

Charterer is the regulated entity directly — no pass-through needed. Cleanest structure regulatorily but rarest commercially.

A negotiated item that's often overlooked: FuelEU compliance surplus ownership. A vessel running B30 generates surplus measurable in tCO₂e with market value (~€185/tCO₂e under Article 21 pooling). Default ownership accrues to the DOC holder; specific allocation needs to be written in.

CII as commercial value signal

CII's direct regulatory consequence is documentary (a SEEMP corrective-action plan if rated D for 3 consecutive years or E for 1 year), not financial. The commercial consequence is bigger: cargo owners and major charterers have started screening on CII rating. A D-rated bulker can be passed over for a C-rated one on the same trade. The rating becomes a commercial gatekeeper, not a regulatory penalty.

The Z-factor tightening trajectory amplifies this. From 5% in 2023 to 21.5% in 2030, the same ship moves down the rating ladder unless it improves. Most existing tonnage will need active intervention — voyage optimization, slow steaming, energy efficiency retrofits — to defend its rating. The commercial value of CII-aware operational data is exactly the difference between staying competitive and getting screened out.
